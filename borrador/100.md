ROLE OF GLUCOSE REGULATION:
Glucose is a monomer that travels through the blood and needs to reach the cells so that they can synthesize ATP and carry out any function.
When we eat a large amount of carbohydrates, they travel to the intestine, where they are broken down into glucose and then enter the bloodstream. They reach the pancreas, which detects a high amount of glucose and secretes a hormone called insulin into the blood. This hormone binds to the membrane receptors of certain organs, takes up the glucose and lets it pass into the cell. These target organs (liver and muscle) will store the extra glucose in the form of glycogen (a glucose polymer). In this way, the blood glucose concentration goes down.
On the other hand, we have glucagon, which acts opposite to glucose. When, in situations such as fasting, the pancreas detects a low blood glucose concentration, it releases glucagon, which travels through the blood to all cells but mainly binds to the liver to break down the stored glucagon and obtain glucose, which would travel through the blood so that everything goes back to normal.
IN TYPE 1 DIABETES: The pancreas stops secreting insulin or does not secrete enough, so the blood glucose concentration is VERY HIGH; therefore, they must inject insulin when they eat, otherwise they can die.
IMPORTANT FACTS TO UNDERSTAND THE PHYSIOLOGY:
The kinetic properties (affinity, inhibition) are determined by its amino acid sequence and its 3D shape.
Glucokinase is found mainly in beta cells, to measure glucose and decide how much insulin to release, and in the liver, to take up glucose after eating and store it as glycogen.
 Glucokinase is a soluble protein found in the cytoplasm. Located in the islets of Langerhans, they are a special site where phosphorylation is also used as a sensor. That is why, when glucokinase fails in MODY2, what you notice is that the pancreas measures glucose incorrectly, even though the rest of the body's cells keep phosphorylating it without any problem with their hexokinase.
PHYSIOLOGY UNDER NORMAL CONDITIONS:
The GCK gene encodes hexokinase 4, which is an isoenzyme and a glucose sensor that catalyzes the phosphorylation of D-glucose to glucose 6-phosphate in the liver and the beta cells of the pancreas. By adding a phosphate group, glucose-6-phosphate can no longer cross the membrane and is trapped inside. Its low affinity for glucose and the absence of product inhibition make it a true sensor of blood glucose concentration.
Phosphorylation consists of adding a phosphate group to the glucose inside the cell, using up one ATP, which becomes ADP; the glucose can then be used for glycolysis, glycogen or other pathways that the cell will decide.
This enzyme has a high Michaelis constant (rate constant), around 8 to 10 mmol/L, which means that it is only activated and works at high speed when blood glucose levels. Unlike the other hexokinases, it is not inhibited by its own product, which allows it to keep converting glucose as long as there is plenty of it.
In the pancreas it acts as a metabolic sensor in B cells; by phosphorylating the glucose that enters the beta cells, glucokinase triggers the metabolic chain that ultimately raises the intracellular ATP/ADP ratio, closes the ATP-sensitive potassium channels and triggers insulin exocytosis. GLUCOKINASE IS A MEMBRANE PROTEIN BUT IT PHOSPHORYLATES GLUCOSE INSIDE THE CELL. Without functional glucokinase, the beta cell loses the ability to adjust insulin secretion according to blood glucose. That is why this enzyme is often called the pancreatic glucose sensor. 
MUTATIONS IN ITS GENE can cause disorders such as MODY2 diabetes (through loss of function) look up whether it is related to one of the two copies being passed on mutated autosomal dominant inheritance.
MODY2 is what happens when the enzyme that phosphorylates glucose in the pancreas fails.
So, once glucose is phosphorylated, the beta cells have to decide how much insulin to release. To do that, they need to “know” how much glucose there is in the blood. They do not have a receptor that measures glucose directly. What they do is more ingenious: let glucose in and measure how fast they phosphorylate it. And this works depending on whether there is enough ATP. The cell knows how much ATP it gets from each glucose.
So why “more phosphorylation = more ATP”
Glucokinase is the slowest step of the chain in the beta cell, so it sets how many glucose molecules enter the pathway per second. It works like a tap:
•	If it phosphorylates faster, more glucose molecules enter the pathway per second.
•	Each one uses 1 ATP when it enters, but gives back about 30.
•	The net result is much more ATP in the cell.
That is why, even though the specific phosphorylation step consumes ATP, speeding it up raises the total ATP.

Glucokinase has low affinity, 8 mmol. Its range compared to other hexokinases is higher, but this has its good and bad side. It is higher, so there will be a higher concentration of glucose inside the cell, and the pancreas interprets that as “I have to release insulin”, but once it reaches that range (the glucose concentration), its phosphorylation rate will increase and go down according to blood glucose.
In MODY2 the person has one mutated copy of the GCK gene and one normal copy.  With the same amount of glucose, less is phosphorylated, less ATP is produced and the cell interprets that there is less glucose than there actually is: it is not that there is less, it is that the measurement comes out too low. With half of the glucokinase functional, the beta cell reads glucose as lower than it is. More blood glucose is needed to reach the same phosphorylation rate and trigger insulin. The result is that the thermostat is shifted upwards. 
 
Why is it measured while fasting?
It is measured this way because it is the resting value: there is no recent food to alter it. That value reflects where the thermostat is set.
Healthy person while fasting: about 4-5.5 mmol/L (70-100 mg/dL).
MODY2 while fasting: about 5.5-8 mmol/L (100-145 mg/dL).
Hyperglycemia simply means “blood glucose above normal”. It is mild because it is only slightly above, not sky-high as in untreated type 1 diabetes (where it can go above 15–20 mmol/L). And the person has the other half of the enzyme working: the system keeps regulating well, just around a somewhat higher value.
In summary: in MODY2, glucose does not arrive in smaller amounts or more slowly. What happens is that the pancreas's “meter” gives readings below reality, so the body keeps glucose a bit higher than normal, in a stable way, and that is what you see when you measure it while fasting.
