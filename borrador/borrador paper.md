**BACKGROUND**
Diabetes is a chronic disease characterized by elevated blood glucose levels. It occurs when the body does not produce enough insulin (a hormone produced by the pancreas that allows glucose from food to enter cells, where it is used to generate energy). Insulin is secreted by the beta cells located in the islets of Langerhans (the endocrine portion of the pancreas). 
MODY2 is a benign genetic condition that raises baseline blood glucose levels. However, because the body is still able to use insulin and transport glucose into cells, the cells do not experience an energy deficit, and the typical destructive complications associated with conventional diabetes do not occur.

The main mechanism involved in diabetes is as follows: insulin has a regulatory function by binding to receptors on target cells (skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and liver cells), allowing glucose to enter the cells.

In type 1 diabetes, the pancreas does not produce enough insulin. As a result, glucose cannot enter the cells, leading to a lack of energy. Extracellularly (in the bloodstream), hyperglycemia develops: glucose accumulates in the blood at very high levels.

In type 2 diabetes, it is the cells that do not respond properly to the hormone; this condition is known as insulin resistance.

**Physiology of wild-type glucokinase:**
Glucokinase (GK, also called hexokinase IV) is an enzyme encoded by the *GCK* gene on chromosome 7p13. It catalyses the first step of glucose metabolism: it adds a phosphate group to glucose, using ATP, to produce glucose-6-phosphate (G6P). It is found mainly in pancreatic beta cells and in liver cells (hepatocytes). The gene has two tissue-specific promoters, so the pancreas and the liver produce slightly different versions (isoforms) of the enzyme (Postic 2001).

Other hexokinases (types I–III) bind glucose very strongly, so they already work at full speed even when there is very little glucose. Glucokinase is different in three ways: it has a low affinity for glucose (it reaches half of its maximum speed at about 8-10 mM), its activity follows an S-shaped (sigmoidal) curve with a Hill coefficient of about 1.7, and it is not significantly inhibited by its product, G6P, at normal concentrations (Matschinsky 1990, Matschinsky 1993). Because of this, the speed of glucokinase changes with blood glucose levels within the normal range. This is why glucokinase works as the body's glucose sensor.

In pancreatic beta cells, glucokinase controls the slowest (rate-limiting) step of glucose metabolism. After a meal, blood glucose rises and glucose enters the beta cell, where glucokinase phosphorylates it. As glucose is broken down through glycolysis and in the mitochondria, the cell produces more ATP. The higher ATP/ADP ratio closes ATP-sensitive potassium (K-ATP) channels in the cell membrane. This changes the electrical charge of the membrane (depolarization), which opens calcium channels. Calcium then enters the cell and causes the release of insulin from storage granules (Thompson 2021). Since glucokinase controls the speed of this whole chain, it sets the blood glucose threshold for insulin release at about 5 mM (Matschinsky 1993).

In the liver, glucokinase helps to take up glucose and store it after meals. During fasting, when glucose is low, another protein called glucokinase regulatory protein (GKRP) binds glucokinase and keeps it inactive inside the nucleus. This stops the liver from using glucose when the body needs it. When blood glucose rises after a meal, glucokinase is released and moves to the cytoplasm, where the G6P it produces is used to make glycogen, the liver's glucose store (Postic 2001, Zhang 2023).

Glucokinase is a monomer (a single protein chain) with two parts, a large domain and a small domain, separated by a cleft where glucose binds. Without glucose, the enzyme stays mostly in an inactive "super-open" shape. When glucose binds, the enzyme slowly changes to an active "closed" shape. This slow change of shape explains why glucokinase shows an S-shaped curve even though it has only one glucose-binding site (Kamata 2004).



**Fisiopatología molecular:**
El desarrollo fisiopatológico del MODY2 viene de mutaciones genéticas que reducen el funcionamiento del gen GCK, el cual codifica para la enzima glucocinasa. En condiciones normales, esta enzima actúa como el principal sensor de glucosa en las células Beta del páncreas. Sin embargo, la mutación en el MODY2 altera la estructura y el comportamiento de la enzima, provocando una disminución en su afinidad por detectar la glucosa. Esto significa que la enzima necesita concentraciones de glucosa más altas para iniciar su actividad.

A nivel molecular, esta menos afinidad enzimática retrasa drásticamente el primer paso del metabolismo celular: la fosforilación de la glucosa a glucosa-6-fosfato. Como consecuencia directa de este retraso, la mitocondria de la célula beta procesa la glucosa a menor velocidad y, por tanto, genera una menor cantidad de energía en forma de ATP ante concentraciones normales de azúcar en sangre. Al no producirse un aumento adecuado en la cantidad de energía dentro de la célula, se interrumpe el proceso eléctrico necesario para la secreción de insulina.

El fallo en la producción de ATP impide el cierre de los canales de potasio (canales K-ATP) ubicados en la membrana celular. Al permanecer abiertos, la célula no retiene las cargas positivas, impidiendo la despolarización de la membrana (que se cargue de electricidad). La ausencia de este cambio eléctrico mantiene cerrados los canales de calcio (Ca2+). Sin la entrada masiva de iones de calcio al interior de la célula, las vesículas que almacenan la insulina no reciben la señal necesaria para unirse a la membrana y liberar la hormona a la sangre.

El resultado global de esta alteración molecular no es una incapacidad total del páncreas para producir o secretar insulina, sino un aumento en el nivel de azúcar necesario para activar el sistema. Mientras que una célula beta sana inicia la liberación de insulina a partir de concentraciones aproximadas de 90 mg/dL de glucosa en sangre, el "sensor descalibrado" de la célula afectada por MODY2 requiere una cantidad de azúcar en sangre mayor (típicamente entre 120 y 130 mg/dL) para forzar a la glucocinasa defectuosa a trabajar. Solo al alcanzar ese nivel elevado se logra producir suficiente ATP para desencadenar la liberación de insulina, lo cual explica los niveles de azúcar permanentemente elevados que presenta el paciente.



**Secondary effects:**
Despite being the most common MODY subtype in countries where routine genetic screenings are performed, the secondary effects of MODY2 and its symptoms are difficult to encounter and detect without proper tests. That being said, mild, benign and asymptomatic hyperglycemia characterise individuals with illness, though normally having no necessity for any pharmacological treatment. The most negative effect it can have is on women during pregnancy due to the fact that the development of the fetus in the case that it has inherited the GSK mutation.

There are different risks involving pregnat women with MODY2. One of these is the increased rate of miscarriage due to the high levels of glucose the fetus is exposed to. While not being wide-reported, there have been cases of congenital malfromations to the offspring of mothers with MODY. Not all mothers with GCK-MODY produce affected affected kids, but these can still be affected to a degree, especially larger than the average. These larger new borns can even exceed those affected with affected mothers, and, evidently, those in which both the mother and the offspring are unaffected.

To prevent these risks, or lower their rate, the main solution is the use of insulin therapy, due to its ability to prevent macrosomia and other effects of MODY2. This type of medication replace the amount of insulin needed that is not being produced, so that the body is not without necessary insulin. That being said, if the fetus has inherited it, since there is a risk of growth restriction, the treatment of maternal hyperglycemia should not be undertaken.

**METHODS**

The human (UniProt P35557) and sheep (NCBI NP_001274400.1) glucokinase sequences were aligned with EMBOSS Needle (EBLOSUM62 matrix, gap open 10, gap extend 0.5) (Madeira 2024). The comparison with *Xenopus laevis* was done with the conservation report provided in class, which includes a sequence alignment and a structural superposition.

**RESULTS**

**Comparison with other species**

Orthologs are the same gene in different species, inherited from a common ancestor. When a position in a protein has not changed during millions of years of evolution, it is probably important for the function of the protein. To study this in glucokinase, we compared the human protein with two other species: *Xenopus laevis* (African clawed frog), an amphibian that is distantly related to humans, and *Ovis aries* (sheep), a mammal that is closely related to humans. We expected the sheep protein to be much more similar to the human protein than the frog protein.

The human and *Xenopus* proteins share 80.6% of their aligned positions, and 88.4% of the positions have amino acids from the same physicochemical group. Most of the differences are found at the beginning of the protein (the frog protein lacks the first seven residues) and in loops, which are flexible regions. Other regions are completely identical, for example residues 161–200. The secondary structure (alpha helices and beta strands) is almost the same in both species. The global RMSD between the two structures is 8.18 Å, which seems high. However, the superposition shows that one domain fits almost perfectly while the other is displaced as a block. This suggests that the two structures are in different conformations (open and closed), and not that the fold is different.

The sheep protein is much more similar to the human protein: a global alignment with EMBOSS Needle gave 96.6% identity and 98.3% similarity, with no gaps (both proteins have 465 residues). Only 16 positions are different, and most of them are changes between similar amino acids. None of them is between positions 309 and 341.

In the three species, position 339 is a glutamate (E). This means that this residue has been conserved in mammals and amphibians, so the E339K mutation changes a position that evolution has not allowed to change. In addition, the local backbone displacement at position 339 between human and *Xenopus* (3.13 Å) is larger than between the wild-type and the mutant (2.49 Å), even though the frog enzyme works normally. This suggests that the problem of E339K is not only the movement of the backbone, but the change of charge from negative to positive, which breaks the interactions of glutamate 339 with His416 and Arg422 (Liu 2011).
