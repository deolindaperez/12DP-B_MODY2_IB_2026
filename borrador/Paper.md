## Genetic and proteomic characterization of the glucokinase E339K variant in maturity-onset diabetes of the young type 2 (MODY2).

[Maeve López Pardo, Deolinda Pérez, Biel Porté, Unai Ros, Ariadna Sospedra]  
[Department of bioinformatics, UPC Campus Nord, Barcelona, Spain]

## Abstract
Human glucokinase (GK) is a dynamic enzyme of 465 amino acids that functions as a beta-cell glucose sensor, regulating insulin secretion and blood glucose homeostasis (Kamata et al., 2004). 
An heterozygous mutation in the GCK gene, which encodes human glucokinase, produces a structural change in the protein, causing Maturity Onset Diabetes of the Young type 2 (MODY2)(Kant et al., 2022)(Tosur & Philipson, 2022). 
In this paper, we identify the mutation as a single nucleotide substitution in GCK gene (c.1015G>A) creating a missense mutation E339K (PDB entry 3QIC)(Liu et al., 2011) (Gonzalez, 2026). 
Glu339 and Lys339 differ chemically in charge, switching from negative to positive; thus breaking a crucial bond with His416 and forcing it to a 90º conformational change toward the active site(Liu et al., 2011) & [5] (Centeno, 2026). 
Furthermore, a phosphorylated Ser411 was also observed in the mutant crystal structure, physically closing the ATP-binding pocket(Liu et al., 2011). 
The resulting mutant protein has its ATP affinity significantly lowered, effectively raising the glucose concentration threshold for beta-cells to secrete insulin. 
Nonetheless, patients with MODY2 retain half of their wild-type glucokinase, which explains the symptom of mild and stable fasting hyperglycemia(Kant et al., 2022), [7] (Timsit et al., 2022).

## Keywords
GCK gene, mutation, Glucokinase, E339K, structural change

## Background
Maturity-onset diabetes of the young type 2 (MODY2), also known as GCK-MODY, is a rare form of diabetes characterised by autosomal dominant inheritance and non-autoimmune β-cell dysfunction. It is typically diagnosed before the age of 25 years following the detection of hyperglycaemia. It exhibits a mild and stable fasting hyperglycaemia that is rarely associated with vascular complications and generally requires no pharmacological treatment beyond lifestyle and dietary measures except in special circumstances, such as pregnancy (Rudland 2019).

The exact prevalence of MODY remains uncertain but it is estimated to account for 1.1 - 6.5% of pediatric diabetes cases, with marked geographical variation largely attributable to differences in the criteria used to identify cases. GCK-MODY, HNF1A-MODY, and HNF4A-MODY account for more than 90% of MODY cases with a genetic confirmation in several studies in the UK, Europe, and the USA. (Tosur y Philipson, 'Precision diabetes', p. 1465.)

The GCK gene encodes glucokinase (GK, also called hexokinase IV), an enzyme of 465 amino acids found mainly in the liver and pancreatic beta cells. Glucokinase catalyses the phosphorylation of glucose into glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) using ATP. In pancreatic beta cells, it acts as a glucose sensor and helps regulate insulin secretion. Unlike other hexokinases, glucokinase has a low affinity for glucose, meaning that its activity increases when glucose levels rise. Mutations that reduce its activity can increase the glucose level needed for insulin secretion, causing the mild hyperglycemia observed in MODY2 (Kamata 2004).

Structurally this protein is composed of two domains, the large one and the small one, which are separated by a deep cleft, forming the active site where the phosphorylation will take place (Kamata 2004). These two domains, once altered by the mutation, play an important role in disrupting the enzyme’s glucose-sensing threshold. GCK-MODY results from heterozygous inactivating variants in GCK. These variants raise the glucose threshold for insulin secretion by pancreatic β-cells and alter hepatic glucose handling, so that glucose is maintained at a slightly elevated but tightly regulated level. In the liver, glucokinase is regulated by the glucokinase regulatory protein (GKRP), which keeps it inactive in the nucleus during fasting and releases it after a meal, when the glucose-6-phosphate it produces is used to synthesise glycogen (Postic 2001).

One of the mutations associated with MODY2 is E339K (c.1015G>A), which replaces glutamate with lysine at position 339 of the protein. Previous studies have shown that this mutation affects the structure and activity of glucokinase. In this study, we aim to analyse the E339K mutation by comparing glucokinase sequences from different species and studying its genetic and structural effects. Our goal is to better understand how this mutation changes the function of glucokinase and contributes to MODY2. (Liu 2011)


## Results  

### Genomics characterization of the E339K variant

Comparison of the provided wild-type and mutant sequences revealed a single nucleotide substitution at position 1015 of the coding sequence (c.1015G>A; reference transcript NM_000162.5). This position corresponds to the first nucleotide of codon 339, which changes from [GAG] to [AAG]. The substitution is a transition (a purine replaced by another purine) and does not alter the reading frame or introduce a premature stop codon. Instead, it changes the encoded amino acid, classifying it as a missense variant (p.Glu339Lys, E339K).

The variant replaces glutamate with lysine at position 339 of the 465-residue protein. These two amino acids have opposite electrostatic properties: glutamate is an acidic residue with a negative charge and can act as a hydrogen-bond acceptor, whereas lysine is a basic residue with a positive charge and can act as a hydrogen-bond donor. The substitution therefore reverses the local charge from −1 to +1. (Ricard Gonzalez, 'personal communication', 2026)

This charge reversal may alter local interactions within the protein, motivating further investigation into the evolutionary conservation and structural environment of residue 339. (Liu 2011)

### Comparison with other species

Orthologs are the same gene in different species, inherited from a common ancestor. When a position in a protein has not changed during millions of years of evolution, it is probably important for the function of the protein.

*Xenopus laevis* (assigned by the instructors) was used as a distant vertebrate and *Ovis aries* as a closely related mammal, so that conservation can be assessed over both short and long evolutionary distances. To assess the evolutionary conservation of residue 339, human glucokinase was compared with its orthologues from *Xenopus laevis* (African clawed frog) and *Ovis aries* (sheep).

The human and *Xenopus* proteins share 80.6% identity, and 88.4% of the aligned positions contain amino acids with similar physicochemical properties. Most of the differences are found at the beginning of the protein (the frog protein lacks the first seven residues) and in flexible loop regions. Other regions are completely identical, including residues 161–200. The secondary structure (alpha helices and beta strands) are also highly similar. The global root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the two structures is 8.18 Å. Although this value is relatively high, the structural superposition shows that one domain aligns closely while the other is displaced as a block. This suggests that the two structures are in different conformations (open and closed), rather than different overall protein folds. (Gonzalez 2026; Centeno 2026)

The sheep protein is much more similar to the human glucokinase. Global alignment with EMBOSS Needle gave 96.6% identity and 98.3% similarity, with no gaps, as both proteins contain 465 residues. Only 16 positions are different, and most of them are changes between similar amino acids. None of them is between positions 309 and 341, the region surrounding residue 339.

In the three species, position 339 is a glutamate (E). This indicates that this residue 339 is conserved in the three species analysed, which include a mammal and an amphibian, suggesting that it is under functional constraint. The local backbone displacement at position 339 is 3.13 Å between human and *Xenopus*, and 2.49 Å between the wild type and the E339K mutant.

### Structural and kinetic impact of GCK E339K mutation

In the wild-type (WT) structure, the glutamate residue at position 339 (Glu339) forms two key hydrogen bonds: one with His416 at a distance of 2.8 Å and another with Arg422 at 3.3 Å. These interactions stabilize helix 9 and help maintain the proper alignment of His416.  

In the E339K mutant, Glu339 is replaced by a positively charged lysine (Lys339), so this stabilizing network is lost. 
Lys339 reorients 180º to form a new hydrogen bond, since two positive charges repel each other (His416 - Lys339); this shortens helix 9 (Figure 1) and destabilizes the surrounding region.
The loss of the original interaction with residue 339 allows the side chain of His416 to rotate by nearly 90º towards the ATP-binding pocket. According to the structural analysis reported by Liu et al. (2011), this rotation creates a physical blockage that may interfere with ATP binding.
It is important to note the use of ATP analog AMP-PNP in the 3D structure instead of native ATP, in order to prevent the catalytic activity of glucokinase during crystalization. 

Additionally, the mutant structure contains a phosphorylated serine residue at position 411 (Ser411). The phosphorylated residue forms a hydrogen bond with Thr82, bringing the surrounding loop approximately 3 Å closer to the large domain. This structural rearrangement further restricts access to the ATP-binding pocket (Figure 2).

These structural modifications are associated with changes in the kinetic properties of the mutant enzyme. The ATP Michaelis constant (Kₘ) increases from 0.32 mM in the wild-type enzyme to 1.50 mM in the E339K mutant, indicating an approximately reduction in ATP affinity. Additionally, the overall relative activity drops by 80%, leaving the mutant enzyme with only 20% of its normal catalytic capacity. The glucose half-saturation concentration (S₀.₅) also increases from 8.65 mM to 15.44 mM. (Liu 2011)

Because the mutant protein loses 80% of its activity, pancreatic cells need higher blood sugar levels than normal to release insulin. This higher limit is the direct cause of high blood sugar (hyperglycemia) in MODY2 patients.

## Discussion

The results of this study connect a single nucleotide change in GCK with the clinical phenotype of MODY2 across several biological levels. At the gene level, the c.1015G>A transition produces a missense change, p.Glu339Lys, which reverses the charge of residue 339 from negative to positive. 
At the structural level, this charge reversal disrupts the hydrogen bonds between Glu339 and His416 and Arg422. His416 then rotates towards the ATP-binding pocket, and phosphorylated Ser411 forms a hydrogen bond with Thr82, pulling the β2–β3 loop about 3 Å towards the large domain, so that access to the nucleotide is impaired by two independent mechanisms (Liu 2011). At the functional level, these changes are consistent with the kinetic data. The ATP Km increases approximately 5-fold, whereas the glucose S0.5 increases less than 2-fold, indicating that the main defect of E339K lies in nucleotide binding rather than glucose binding. This agrees with the location of both structural perturbations in the ATP-binding region.

MODY2 patients are heterozygous and retain one wild-type allele. Because glucokinase is a monomer, the mutant protein is not expected to impair the wild-type enzyme, so the two forms are likely to contribute independently to total activity. Total glucokinase activity in beta cells would therefore be only partially reduced. In beta cells, glucokinase controls the rate of glycolysis, and the resulting increase in the ATP/ADP ratio closes ATP-sensitive potassium (K-ATP) channels, which depolarises the membrane and triggers insulin release (Thompson 2021). A partial loss of glucokinase activity reduces ATP production at any given glucose concentration, so that a higher blood glucose level is needed to reach the threshold for insulin secretion. The glucose-sensing system is not abolished but recalibrated: glucose homeostasis is preserved at a higher set point. This explains why patients present with mild, stable fasting hyperglycaemia rather than progressive diabetes, consistent with the typical range of approximately 5.5-8 mM reported for GCK-MODY (Timsit 2022).

The cross-species comparison supports the functional importance of residue 339. Glu339 is conserved in human, sheep and Xenopus laevis, species separated by approximately 350 million years of evolution.  Additionally, the local backbone displacement at position 339 between human and *Xenopus* (3.13 Å) is larger than between the wild-type and the mutant (2.49 Å). This suggests that the functional effects of E339K do not depend only on backbone displacement but also on the reversal of the residue's charge and its interactions with surrounding amino acids.
In substitution matrices such as BLOSUM62, used in our alignment, a glutamate-to-lysine exchange has a positive score (+1), so it is often tolerated between homologous proteins; however, Glu339 is invariant in the three species. This responds to the main question addressed in this work: a sequence that appears chemically moderate can have a strong functional effect when it disrupts a conserved network of interactions, even at a position distant from the active site.

From a clinical perspective, the mild and stable phenotype of MODY2 has consequences. Because hyperglycaemia is rarely associated with vascular complications, most patients do not require pharmacological treatment. However. GCK-MODY is frequently misdiagnosed as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, which can lead to unnecesary lifelong treatment. Genetic testing in therefore essential to establish the correct diagnosis and avoid inappropriate therapy.


This study has a several limitations. The structural and kinetic data were obtained from a single study and were measured in vitro using recombinant enzyme expressed in Escherichia coli, which may not fully reflect glucokinase behaviour in human beta cells. The phosphorylation of Ser411 was observed in this recombinant protein, and its origin and physiological relevance remain unclear. The cross-species structural comparison was based on a single pair of structures that appear to be in different conformations, which inflates the global RMSD. Finally, fasting glucose values specific to E339K carriers were not analysed, and the clinical range used corresponds to GCK-MODY in general. Future work could include kinetic and structural analyses in a cellular context and a clinical data from E339K carriers.



## Conclusions

This study shows that the E339K variants explains the molecular basis of MODY2 through a chain of effects linking gene, protein structure and function. A single nucleotide transition in GCK (c.1015G>A) reverses the charge of a conserved residue, disrupting a hydrogen-bond network that mantais the position of His416. Together with the phosphorylation of Ser411, this impairs access to the ATP-binding pocket, so that the mutant enzyme mainly loses its ability to bind ATP rather than glucose. In heterozygous patients, the remaining wild-type allele preserves glucose sensing, but at a higher threshold, which accounts for the mild and stable hyperglycaemia characteristic of the disease.

Comparison between the wild-type and mutant forms identifies structural changes responsible for the loss of function, while comparison across species shows that residue 339 has been conserved from amphibians to mammals, supporting its functional importance. More generally, these results show that the effect of a substitution depends on its structural context: a chemically moderate change at a position distant from the active site can severely impair enzyme function when it disrupts a conserved network of interactions. Combining sequence conservation with structural and kinetic analysis therefore provides a useful framework for interpreting disease-associated variants.

## Methods
The wild-type and mutant human glucokinase FASTA sequences were provided as part of the course materials. The *Xenopus laevis* orthologue (NCBI RefSeq NP_001079298.1) was assigned by the instructors as a distant vertebrate, and *Ovis aries* was added as a closely related mammal. The human glucokinase sequence was obtained from UniProt (P35557), and the sheep sequence from NCBI (NP_001274400.1). A conservation report provided in class included sequence and structural comparisons between wild-type human glucokinase, the E339K variant (PDB ID: 3QIC) and the *Xenopus laevis* orthologue. The wild-type reference structures were 1V4S (closed, glucose-bound) and 1V4T (super-open) (Kamata 2004).

The wild-type and mutant nucleotide sequences were compared using BLASTn to identify the c.1015G>A substitution. Orthologues were identified with BLASTp against the NCBI RefSeq protein database, using the human sequence (P35557) as query; for sheep, the curated RefSeq record was selected. The human and sheep protein sequences were aligned using EMBOSS Needle with the EBLOSUM62 substitution matrix, a gap opening penalty of 10 and a gap extension penalty of 0.5 (Madeira 2024). The comparison with *Xenopus laevis* was based on the sequence alignment and structural superposition included in the course conservation report.

The three-dimensional structure of the E339K variant was identified using the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) Sequence Search tool with the mutated human glucokinase sequence and a sequence identity threshold of 100%. Protein structures were superimposed, and their secondary structures were assigned using ChimeraX in the conservation report prepared by the course instructors (Gonzalez 2026). Structural differences were evaluated using the global Cα root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and per-residue backbone displacement. Sequence conservation was assessed by comparing identical and similar amino acid positions across species.

Kinetic parameters, including glucose S₀.₅, ATP Kₘ and relative enzymatic activity, as well as information about the structural mechanisms involving His416 and the Ser411–Thr82 interaction, were obtained from the primary study by Liu et al. (2011), using its text, figures and figure legends. These values were used to compare wild-type glucokinase with the E339K variant. No additional molecular modelling, new kinetic experiments or statistical tests were performed.
